1 / Context

A central problem in computational immunology

  1. 1MHC class I proteins bind short protein fragments ("peptides") from inside each cell.
  2. 2They present these peptides on the cell surface for inspection by T-cells.
  3. 3T-cells detect abnormal peptides from virus-infected or cancer-mutated cells and trigger their destruction.

Why computational prediction matters

Predicting which peptides a given MHC class I protein will present is the foundation of neoantigen prioritisation and immunopeptidomics research, upstream of experimental validation.

2 / Problem framing

Computationally, this is a sequence-ranking problem

Ranking under a context key

A recurring ML pattern: score many short candidates for one fixed conditioner.

Input / output

Conditioner
One HLA allele (34-residue pseudo-sequence)
Candidates
102 – 104 peptide sequences (8–11 aa)
Output
Rank by BA + EL → fusion

Why ranking fidelity matters

Small ranking errors at the top compound into large experimental waste. The pipeline is rate-limited by the false-positive rate of the upstream ranker.

4 / The evaluation problem

Why reported gains in this field are hard to trust

① Train–test leakage

NetMHCpan's public training corpus contains ~160 K peptide–HLA pairs. Most benchmarks share rows with that corpus.

If the overlap is not removed, your "test set" is partly a training set, and reported gains are not generalisation.

Kapoor & Narayanan 2023  ·  Bernett et al. 2024

② Unadjusted multiple testing

Benchmarks score across dozens of HLA alleles; each treated as independent.

With 100 alleles and α = 0.05, ~5 chance wins — often reported as evidence of superiority.

Standard FWER correction (Holm 1979) is widely ignored in this literature.

Contamination ledger: TransPHLA shares 26.9% of rows with NetMHCpan-4.1 training corpus; IEDB auto_bench 15–22%.

5 / Our evaluation contract

The three-condition superiority rule

A signed Δ (DeepNeo − baseline) is reported as a real win only if all three hold:

  1. 1Paired DeLong p < 0.05 on the same rows.
  2. 2Per-allele Wilcoxon signed-rank matches the pooled Δ sign, with Holm-adjusted p < 0.05 across the family of four TransPHLA comparison rows.
  3. 3Allele-clustered bootstrap 95% CI on Δ excludes zero (B = 1,000; n = 118,143 rows).

Preceded by exact-pair decontamination

Every evaluation row that also appears in NetMHCpan-4.1's training-pair union is removed before scoring. On TransPHLA: 43,509 of 161,652 rows removed (26.9%) → 118,143 clean rows.

9 / Case study · evaluation-leakage failure

A documented transductive-overfitting failure

We fit a second-stage pocket rescorer by 5-fold cross-validation on the evaluation benchmark itself.

0.862
in-sample AUROC
(transductive)
→
0.442
held-out alleles
(inductive) · below chance

What happened

Using evaluation-set labels to fit an inference-time head is a form of leakage that ordinary train/test hygiene does not catch. The apparent gain is in-sample; the collapse is consistent with evaluation-set fitting, not transferable discrimination.

The component is withdrawn from all deployment claims and reported here as a documented negative for inference-time leakage.

Source: supplementary_data/inductive_reranker_result.json · See also Kapoor 2023, Rosenblatt 2024, Bernett 2024, Joeres 2025.

10 / Takeaway

What this paper actually claims

① Model

A compact 35 M-parameter PLM for peptide–MHC class I ranking, trained on the official NetMHCpan-4.1 folds.

② Protocol

A leakage-aware, multiplicity-controlled evaluation contract, applied verbatim in Results and ligated to the methodology literature cited.

③ Negatives

Two concrete evaluation-failure case studies on our own data — a successor-corpus holdout overlap and a transductive-overfitting rescorer collapse.

Scope restrictions

Class I MHC  ·  canonical 8–11-mer peptides  ·  no clinical, Class II, PTM, or TCR-specificity claim.
